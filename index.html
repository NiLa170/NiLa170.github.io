San Francisco Crime Story

Year 2020: Theft and larceny patterns temporarily drops in heavily populated San Fracisco

An exploration of how COVID-19 disrupted crime patterns in San Francisco

This article draws on a dataset of reported crime incidents in San Francisco. For clarity, the analysis focuses on a subset of categories referred to as Personal Focus Crimes. This includes assault, robbery, larceny/theft, and many more.

Within this group, larceny/theft stands out as the most frequent category. It includes a wide range of offenses, from pickpocketing to car break-ins, and is often closely tied to the rhythms of urban life. What is particularly surprising is the jump regarding the number of larceny/theft incidents reported from 2019 to 2020.

This dramatic shift—the largest single-year change in the dataset—coincides with the first year of COVID-19 shutdowns. It is this disruption, and what it reveals about the relationship between the pandemic crisis and crimes like larceny/theft, that forms the starting point of the story that follows.

Drops in reported incidents - What happened?

The number of incidents fell from 44,749 in 2019 to 28,727 in 2020—a dramatic 35.8% drop. This is a very significant break in the pattern looking at the overall trend over the past two decades.

The timing is precise. Monthly counts begin to collapse in March 2020: from 3,270 incidents in February to 2,387 in March and just 1,788 in April. The question is what had changed during that period.

Why this shift?

To understand this shift, it helps to consider where theft usually happens. The following map summarizes where theft has historically concentrated. Hotspots cluster in central commercial and visitor-heavy areas, consistent with places that usually have high foot traffic, parked cars, shopping, and tourism. In a normal year, these are high-opportunity environments for larceny.

In 2020, those same areas saw the largest declines. Central districts dropped by more than half, mirroring the disappearance of daily activity. Offices emptied, tourism collapsed, and public life retreated indoors.

The change was not only spatial, but temporal. Before 2020, theft followed a familiar daily rhythm, peaking particularly in the evening between 5 and 9pm. In 2020, that rhythm flattened showing two very small peaks, one around noon and one around 6pm.

Incidents became more evenly distributed across the second half of the day, with a slightly higher share occurring overnight. One way to read this is that routine daytime movement patterns were disrupted, and theft opportunities changed with them.

Possible causes

The explanation likely lies in a combination of forces, all converging at the same moment. On March 16, 2020, San Francisco issued a shelter-in-place order. Non-essential businesses closed, travel was restricted, and residents were told to stay home.

Almost immediately, the city emptied. MTC (Metropolitan Transportation Commission) transit statistics show weekday BART (Bay Area Rapid Transit) exits plunging from 409,515 (March 2019) to 166,574 (March 2020), and from 414,397 (April 2019) to 25,136 (April 2020). Tourism followed the same trajectory, cutting the number of daily visitors by more than half. Fewer people in public space meant fewer opportunities for theft—fewer cars to break into, fewer distracted pedestrians, and fewer crowded environments.

Importantly, this was not unique to San Francisco. FBI national statistics report a decline in property crime (and specifically larceny-theft) from 2019 to 2020. That broader background trend is consistent with what we observe locally in SF.

Reopening and rise of incidents

As the city gradually reopened in 2021 and 2022, theft began to rise again. This rebound reinforces the idea that larceny is closely linked to routine activity: when people return, so do the conditions that make these crimes possible.

Still, it is important to recognize what the data can and cannot tell us. These are police-reported incidents, shaped not only by behavior but also by reporting practices and enforcement patterns. Even so, the scale and timing of the 2020 drop point clearly to one conclusion:

What can we conclude?

When a city’s daily life pauses, its crime patterns can change just as dramatically.
