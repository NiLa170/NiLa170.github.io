San Francisco Crime Story

Why Did Larceny/Theft Drop So Sharply from 2019 to 2020?

An exploration of how a city’s routines vanished—and took crime patterns with them

In San Francisco’s crime data, one category dominates the story: larceny and theft. For years, it has been the city’s most common crime—steady, persistent, and deeply tied to everyday urban life. Then, in 2020, something unusual happened.

The number of incidents fell from 44,749 in 2019 to 28,727 in 2020—a dramatic 35.8% drop. In a dataset that typically moves gradually, this is not just a fluctuation. It is a break in the pattern.

The timing is precise. Monthly counts begin to collapse in March 2020: from 3,270 incidents in February to 2,387 in March and just 1,788 in April. The question is not whether something changed—but what.

To understand this shift, it helps to consider where theft usually happens. In a typical year, incidents cluster in dense, central parts of the city—places filled with shoppers, commuters, and tourists. These are environments rich in opportunity: parked cars, busy sidewalks, and constant movement.

In 2020, those same areas saw the largest declines. Central districts dropped by more than half, mirroring the disappearance of daily activity. Offices emptied, tourism collapsed, and public life retreated indoors.

The change was not only spatial, but temporal. Before 2020, theft followed a familiar daily rhythm, peaking in the afternoon and early evening. In 2020, that rhythm flattened.

Incidents became more evenly distributed across the day, with a slightly higher share occurring overnight. This suggests that the routines shaping everyday life—and crime opportunities—had been disrupted.

The explanation likely lies in a combination of forces, all converging at the same moment. On March 16, 2020, San Francisco issued a shelter-in-place order. Non-essential businesses closed, travel was restricted, and residents were told to stay home.

Almost immediately, the city emptied. Transit use plummeted, with BART ridership falling sharply between March and April. Tourism followed the same trajectory, cutting the number of daily visitors by more than half. Fewer people in public space meant fewer opportunities for theft—fewer cars to break into, fewer distracted pedestrians, fewer crowded environments.

The data reflects this directly. One major subtype—theft from locked vehicles over $950—dropped by nearly 50% in a single year. Given how much of San Francisco’s larceny is tied to vehicles, this alone explains a large share of the overall decline.

Importantly, this was not unique to San Francisco. Across the United States, property crime declined in 2020, suggesting a broader shift in behavior tied to the pandemic.

“This was not a sudden solution to crime—it was a sudden disappearance of opportunity.”

As the city gradually reopened in 2021 and 2022, theft began to rise again. This rebound reinforces the idea that larceny is closely linked to routine activity: when people return, so do the conditions that make these crimes possible.

Still, it is important to recognize what the data can and cannot tell us. These are police-reported incidents, shaped not only by behavior but also by reporting practices and enforcement patterns. Even so, the scale and timing of the 2020 drop point clearly to one conclusion:

When a city’s daily life pauses, its crime patterns can change just as dramatically.
